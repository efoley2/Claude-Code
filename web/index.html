If you already installed an accessibility widget

You may have bought one after the last time this came up. Here is what has since become public about that category.

  • 38.5% of businesses sued in 2025 already had an accessibility overlay installed. Having one is not correlated with being left alone. In several filings the overlay itself was described as an additional barrier.
  • The FTC fined accessiBe $1 million in January 2025 over deceptive claims that its AI-powered widget made customer websites compliant, and over undisclosed paid reviews.
  • Overlays do not work, and the reason is structural. A script that loads on top of your theme cannot know what your product photo actually shows, cannot restructure a checkout flow that makes no sense read aloud in order, and cannot repair a button your theme's JavaScript renders unreachable by keyboard. It patches the surface at runtime. The code underneath is unchanged, and the code underneath is what a tester documents.
  • Screen reader users largely turn them off. Hundreds of accessibility practitioners, including many who use assistive technology daily, have signed a public statement against overlay products. Source: overlayfactsheet.com

Curbcut does not sell an overlay, a widget, a script, a badge, or a seal. There is nothing to install on your store. We run a scan from outside, on publicly reachable pages only, and hand you a document.

What Curbcut does

A free scanner will hand you 400 undifferentiated violations sorted by WCAG severity, which buries the unlabelled size dropdown on your product page underneath fifty landmark warnings. Curbcut re-sorts that list on three axes.

1. Ranked by what gets cited

About 150 automated rules are mapped to how frequently that specific barrier is named in filed ADA website complaints. Missing alt text, unlabelled dropdowns and empty buttons come first, because that is what complaints actually enumerate.

2. Weighted by where it sits

The same contrast failure scores twice as high on your checkout button as in your footer. A barrier that blocks a purchase is a different thing from a barrier on your shipping-policy page, and it is treated as one.

3. Fixes for your platform

“Products → select product → click the image → set Alt text” for Shopify. “Media Library → select image → fill in Alt text” for WooCommerce. Not a link to a W3C specification page.

4. Deduplicated, then dated

One rule failing on five pages is one template fix, shown once, scored at its worst location. Every report is dated, which gives you a record of when you looked and what you did about it.

What this does not do

This section is the reason the product exists. Read it before you buy, not after.

  • It does not certify compliance. No such certificate exists under the ADA — not from us, not from any vendor, not from any auditor. Anyone selling you one is selling you a document with no legal standing.
  • It does not find everything. Automated testing catches roughly 30–60% of WCAG issues. Whether your alt text is accurate, whether your focus order makes sense, whether your error messages are understandable — a machine cannot judge any of that. A clean Curbcut report would not mean your site is accessible.
  • It does not fix anything on its own. The report tells you what is broken and how to repair it. Someone still has to go and do it — you, your developer, or us under a separate remediation engagement.
  • It does not replace testing with real people. The only way to know how your store behaves for someone using a screen reader is to have someone using a screen reader go through it. If that is what you need, we will tell you so and point you at people who do it.
  • It does not see anything behind a login. We scan publicly reachable pages only, we respect robots.txt, and we never attempt an authenticated area. Your logged-in account pages are not covered.
  • It does not protect you from anything. No software product does. What it does is find and prioritise real barriers so that fixing them is tractable instead of overwhelming.
  • It is not legal advice. We are not lawyers. If you have received correspondence about your website, talk to an attorney about that correspondence. We can tell you what is technically broken and how to repair it; that is the whole of our competence.

1. Images must have alternate text

  • Most-cited barrier
  • WCAG 1.1.1
  • 14 elements
  • image-alt

Why this matters: Missing alternative text is the single most frequently cited barrier in ADA website complaints. A screen reader announces the filename or nothing at all.

Found on 4 pages — worst instance: /products/merino-crew (product page)

How to fix — Shopify · minutes

  1. Products → select product → click the image → set “Alt text”. This flows through every template automatically.
  2. For theme images (banners, logos, icons): Online Store → Themes → Edit code, then find the <img> tag and add an alt attribute.
  3. Describe the image's purpose, not its appearance. For a product photo, the product name is usually right.

2. Select elements must have an accessible name

  • Most-cited barrier
  • WCAG 4.1.2
  • 3 elements
  • select-name

Why this matters: Unlabelled dropdown menus are specifically and repeatedly named in ADA web complaints — size and quantity selectors are the classic example.

Found on 2 pages — worst instance: /cart (cart)

How to fix — Shopify · minutes

  1. Give every <select> a visible <label for="…"> pointing at its id.
  2. Where the design has no room for a visible label, use aria-label — but a visible one is better for everybody.

Straight answers

Will this stop me getting a demand letter?

No, and be suspicious of anyone who says their product will. What fixing real barriers does is make your store usable by more people and give you a dated record of the work. Those are the honest benefits. Everything beyond that is a question for a lawyer.

Am I legally required to do any of this?

That is a legal question about your specific business and we are not qualified to answer it. Ask an attorney. We can tell you what a scanner finds and how to fix it.

I already have a widget. Should I remove it?

We are not going to tell you to rip something out sight unseen. What we will do is scan the site with it installed and show you what remains broken underneath, which is usually the more useful thing to know first.

How is this different from running Lighthouse myself?

It is the same underlying engine — axe-core, which is free and open source, and we say so plainly. The difference is entirely in the ranking, the purchase-path weighting, and the fix instructions written for your platform. If you are technical enough to read raw axe output and prioritise it yourself, you genuinely do not need us.

What if the scan comes back clean?

Then you get a report that says automated testing found nothing, alongside a plain statement that this does not mean the site is accessible, and we tell you the useful next step is manual testing rather than another scan. We would rather say that than invent findings.

Do you scan sites without being asked?

We run scans on publicly reachable pages to prepare outreach, using a user agent that identifies itself as Curbcut, respecting robots.txt, and never touching anything behind a login. If you would rather we did not scan your site, say so and we will remove it permanently.
